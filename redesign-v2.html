FUEL Up

A receipt and claims app used by vehicle operators and the finance team in the Air Power Generation Command. An operator photographs a fuel receipt, an AI model reads it and fills the claim fields, and every receipt lands in one store the finance team can audit and read for spending trends.

A claim only becomes a number after somebody has typed a receipt into a form twice.

  1. The person. Receipt and claims handling ran on manual submission and manual checking. An operator working away from base could not hand a paper receipt in without travelling back to do it, and the finance team lost around two hours a sitting sorting and checking the stack by hand.
  2. Framing. I read it as a digitalisation gap rather than a process one: the work itself was sound, but every step of it ran on paper. I was asked to design and build the platform that replaced it: submission, validation, tracking and reporting in one workflow, on Power Platform with SharePoint as the document store. Add: what you considered and rejected
  3. My role. Lead developer & solution architect. I architected the solution and wrote it. I modelled the claim schema, built the submission and validation flow, implemented authentication and role-based permissions, wired the SharePoint integration, and added the audit trail and the reporting views on top. One week, with a team of seven: two developers, a product owner, two vehicle operators and two people from the finance team.
  4. Iteration. The first version only captured receipts — scan the image, extract the fields, store the record — because I assumed the job was getting paper into a table. What the records were good for once they accumulated changed the scope: read across a period, they show a vehicle's fuel consumption drifting upward as it wears, and they give the finance team something to forecast against rather than only account for. Trend reporting became part of the product rather than a dashboard bolted on afterwards. The obstacle I handled alone was model cost. I tested several models, then simulated a full day of scanning volume against each one's pricing before choosing, so the running cost was a decision rather than a surprise.
  5. Validation. Twenty-five people — a mix of finance staff and vehicle operators — ran the beta before rollout. Both groups use it now, and the product owner signed it off.
  6. What I’d do differently. I would rebuild it spec-first rather than vibe-coding it in Google AI Studio. The speed was real, and so were the gaps it left behind.

Owning a feature means finishing it. Owning a platform means deciding what it will still be able to carry in two years.

Vibe Coding Masterclass

A large-scale masterclass on vibe-coding (directing AI coding agents from a spec to a working app, and reviewing everything they produce), run for a mixed cohort of citizen developers and engineers, and co-delivered with external academic institutions.

The fastest way to find out whether you actually understand a practice is to stand in front of a room and teach it.

  1. The person. RSAF personnel and staff from Nanyang Polytechnic, most of them not from a technical background. The vocabulary was the barrier before the tooling was: people who could not follow the lingo lost interest before they got far enough to find out whether the practice was any use to them.
  2. Framing. I decided the gap was confidence and vocabulary rather than tooling — the software was not hard to get hold of, but the language around it made people assume the practice was not for them. The syllabus covered prompt-driven development, rapid prototyping and Power Apps deployment. Add: what you considered and rejected
  3. My role. Lead instructor. I organised and led it. I wrote the training materials and the hands-on labs, sequenced the syllabus, coordinated delivery with the external academic partners, and taught the sessions. Run on 6 August for a cohort of 170. Add: the year
  4. Iteration. Three things changed between the first run and the ones after it. I had not said clearly enough what attendees needed to bring or install, so later runs stated the device and the software up front. The room was too shy to ask out loud, so I added an anonymous question channel — and the questions got better as soon as nobody had to put their name to one. And nobody left the first run with a record of it, so later runs ended with a written summary to recap against. The obstacle I handled alone was holding a non-technical room's attention. I broke each demonstration into its smallest parts and used the AI chat panel in the IDE to show what the agent was doing and which tools it reached for, so people could learn from the before and after of an action rather than from a description of it.
  5. Validation. External academic institutions co-delivered the training rather than attending it. Internally the masterclass was recognised in PPCoE updates, and attendance held across the cohort.
  6. What I’d do differently. I would drop the live terminal walkthrough. Pasting a documentation URL into the AI chat and letting the room watch it work keeps everyone on one idea, instead of tracking what I had just clicked.
Human need
See beat 1 (the person) above.
What the AI does in the product
The training does not ship a product with AI in it. AI is the subject it teaches.
What I did
See beat 3 (my role) above.
What exists now
A written syllabus and a set of hands-on labs that outlast the event, and a cohort who have each built something with AI-assisted tooling rather than read about it.
